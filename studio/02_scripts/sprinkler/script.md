## 1 ORANGE — 135°F / 57°C — Ordinary
- **o1** Orange. The lowest number in the whole system: 135 degrees Fahrenheit. [T01]
- **o2** That little glass tube is filled with liquid, with one small bubble left inside on purpose. As the air around it heats up, the liquid expands and squeezes the bubble smaller and smaller. [M01]
- **o3** When the bubble has nowhere left to go, the pressure shatters the glass, the plug above it falls out, and water hits the deflector. [M01]
- **o4** Orange heads are meant for ceilings that never get hotter than 100 degrees. The rating sits far enough above that, that a hot day can't reach it, but a fire can. The bubble isn't a flaw. It's the fuse. [T01]

## 2 RED — 155°F / 68°C — Ordinary
- **r1** Red. 155 degrees. The same ordinary class as orange, and the head you're most likely to find in offices and homes. [T01 note]
- **r2** But heat isn't the only thing that can break the glass. On October 20, 2017, a high school cadet staying at a Holiday Inn in Dumfries, Virginia, hung his uniform on the sprinkler in his room. [X03]
- **r3** The bulb broke. Water flooded 10 rooms, the kitchen and the dining area, and a lawsuit later asked for nearly $700,000. The glass can't tell the difference between a fire and a coat hanger. [X03]

## 3 YELLOW — 175°F / 79°C — Intermediate
- **y1** Yellow. 175 degrees, and the start of the intermediate class. [T02]
- **y2** Intermediate heads are for ceilings that get hot on their own. Under a skylight in direct sun, or in an attic below a bare roof, the air at the ceiling can climb well past 100 degrees with no fire anywhere. [U01]
- **y3** Yellow heads are rated for ceilings up to 150 degrees. In a room like that, an orange bulb wouldn't be waiting for a fire. It would be waiting for a sunny afternoon. [T02]

## 4 GREEN — 200°F / 93°C — Intermediate
- **g1** Green. 200 degrees. The same class as yellow, built for the same kind of space, just with more room to spare. [T02]
- **g2** That's the pattern behind the whole code. Each class is built for a hotter ceiling, and its bulbs break comfortably above it. [T01–T05 max ceiling column]
- **g3** And 200 is lower than you might think. A green bulb breaks before a pot of water would even start to boil. [212°F boiling point]

## 5 BLUE — 286°F / 141°C — High
- **b1** Blue. 286 degrees. This is the high class, and it's a big jump. [T03]
- **b2** Blue heads are built for ceilings that can sit at up to 225 degrees, like the space right around an industrial unit heater, where hot air blows straight up at the ceiling all day. In a space like that, a red bulb wouldn't be protection. It would be a leak waiting to happen. [T03, U01]

## 6 PURPLE — 360°F / 182°C — Extra high
- **p1** Purple. 360 degrees. The extra high class. Some manufacturers call this color mauve. [T04]
- **p2** And this is where the code gets confusing. Glass bulbs aren't the only thing that's color coded. On many sprinklers the metal frame is painted too, and the frame code is a completely different list. [T07, medium → "on many sprinklers"]
- **p3** In that list, a red frame means extra high, the same class as this purple bulb. A red bulb means ordinary. Same color, opposite ends of the scale. [T07]

## 7 BLACK — 400°F+ — Very extra high & ultra high
- **k1** Black. The only color that isn't one number. [T05]
- **k2** Black covers two whole classes, very extra high and ultra high, everything from 400 degrees up to 650. One common black rating is 500. [T05, T06]
- **k3** These go in spaces that run hot all day, near ovens, heaters and industrial processes, where anything less would open on its own. Black isn't a warning about the sprinkler. It's a warning about the room. [U02 generic wording]

## 8 THIN BULB — Quick response
- **t1** The thin bulb. Two red bulbs can have exactly the same rating and still not be the same. [M03]
- **t2** Standard response heads use a bulb about 5 millimeters thick. Quick response heads use one about 3 millimeters thick. [M03]
- **t3** Less glass and less liquid means it heats up faster, so it breaks sooner, while the fire is still small. That's why quick response heads go where saving lives is the main job, like offices and schools. The color tells you when it breaks. The thickness tells you how fast it gets there. [M03]

## 9 NO BULB — Fusible link
- **f1** No bulb at all. Some sprinklers hold the water back with a metal link instead: two small plates joined by solder that melts at the rated temperature. [M04]
- **f2** This is the older idea. On August 11, 1874, a New Haven piano maker named Henry Parmelee patented what's considered the first practical automatic sprinkler head. He built it to protect his piano factory, and to lower his insurance bill. [H01]
- **f3** The first automatic sprinkler wasn't built to save lives. It was built to save money. [H01]

## 10 ONE
- **n1** And finally, the most important number on this list isn't a temperature. It's one.
- **n2** In movies, one sprinkler goes off and the whole ceiling opens. That's not how it works. In a normal wet pipe system, every head is on its own, and only the ones that get hot enough break. Smoke doesn't set them off. Heat does. [S01]
- **n3** In U.S. building fires from 2015 to 2019 where sprinklers operated, just one sprinkler operated 77 percent of the time. Five or fewer were enough 97 percent of the time. [S02]
- **n4** And where sprinklers were installed, the death rate per fire was close to 90 percent lower. [S03]
- **n5** On February 20, 2003, 100 people died in The Station nightclub in West Warwick, Rhode Island. The building was old enough that it had never been required to have sprinklers. That same year, Rhode Island required them in every nightclub that holds 150 people or more. [X02]
- **n6** There is one exception. In a deluge system, every head is open, and when it fires, they all discharge at once. Everywhere else, the movies are wrong. [S01]
